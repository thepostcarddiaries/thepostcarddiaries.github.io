Kamakura: Temples, Street Food and Sea Breezes

Kamakura and Sagami Bay through the trees at Hasedera

Kamakura is a picturesque seaside town south of Tokyo, known for its Great Buddha and beautiful surrounding temples. With its leafy green hills and sea views, it makes the perfect day trip escape from the city. We arrived early for a full day of exploring: cute craft shops, the Great Buddha, Hasedera Temple and its gardens, matcha treats, street food along Komachi-dori.

Cute shops on the way

Kamakura starts charming you the moment you step off the train. The street from Hase Station is lined with small boutiques selling local crafts, many with their own handmade signs and personalised touches out the front.

A paper lantern on the street from Hase Station

As we strolled down the street, woven baskets, hand-painted wooden stools and other handmade crafts spilled right out onto the footpath.

Hand-painted stools and woven baskets outside a craft shop

The Great Buddha (Kotoku-in)

Our first stop was the Great Buddha (Daibutsu) at Kotoku-in Temple. This giant bronze statue of Amida Buddha stands over 11 metres tall and dates back to the 1250s. It once sat inside a huge wooden hall, but storms and a tsunami destroyed the building centuries ago, so it has sat out in the open ever since. Photos really don't do its size justice.

The Great Buddha at Kotoku-in

Hasedera Temple

Next we walked over to Hasedera, which you can't miss thanks to the giant red lantern at its gate. The grounds are massive, spread across a hillside, with stairs and winding paths leading you up past shrines, halls and gardens. You could honestly spend hours here, and we did! There's so much to explore, and you can go at your own pace.

Hasedera's gate and its giant red lantern

Rows of tiny Jizo

One of the most moving parts of Hasedera is the rows and rows of tiny stone Jizo statues. Jizo is a Buddhist figure who watches over children (and travellers too). Parents place these little statues here to remember and pray for children they have lost, often babies lost before birth. Walking past hundreds of these small stone figures, each one representing the love and care for someone lost, was something we'll never forget.

Rows of tiny stone Jizo

In front of one of the bigger Jizo there's a little basin of water with a long bamboo ladle. Visitors scoop up the water and gently pour it over the statue as an offering. It's a quiet, beautiful ritual to watch.

A Jizo statue with its bamboo water ladle

Wishes and fortunes

In front of one of the halls, visitors write their wishes on colourful cards and hang them up. They're written in so many different languages, from visitors all around the world. We wrote our own wish and tied it up with the others. We also drew our fortunes (omikuji). If you get a bad one, you tie it up at the temple and leave the bad luck behind, which is exactly what we did!

Wish cards hung up in front of the hall

There are spots for prayer and offerings all through the grounds. The main hall is home to a towering gilded statue of Kannon, the goddess of mercy, and the Amida Hall holds a beautiful golden seated Buddha.

A quiet hall tucked among the trees at Hasedera

The view from the top

One of the highlights of Hasedera was making our way up the stone paths to the lookout at the top. Peeking through the trees, you can see the rooftops of Kamakura, the beach and the sparkling water of Sagami Bay spread out below. It's a beautiful view, and well worth the climb.

Looking out over Kamakura and the beach from Hasedera's gardens

We took in the sights one last time as we made our way back down through the gardens, past ponds filled with lotus leaves and colourful koi gliding through the shallows, with stone lanterns tucked in among the greenery.

A lotus pond with a stone lantern

Colourful koi in the temple gardens

Back near the entrance, we paused to admire the Shoin, a traditional hall with a peaceful, beautifully kept raked gravel garden in front.

The Shoin and its raked gravel garden

Soft moss and a big garden stone

Then, on our way out, I collected a stamp for my stamp book. Hasedera is one of many places across Japan with a free stamp, so if you're a collector too, keep an eye out for it near the entrance.

A refreshing matcha break

Once we'd finished exploring the temples we wanted to see, we stopped for a cool matcha treat and a break from the heat. We enjoyed a matcha soft serve and a matcha Mont Blanc, topped with delicate strands of matcha cream. Both were delicious and so refreshing.

Matcha soft serve (left) and matcha Mont Blanc (right)

After our little pit stop, we continued exploring the peaceful, scenic streets of Kamakura, with traditional houses and quiet lanes lined with trees, and the occasional local slowly cycling by.

A quiet back street, with a local cycling by

Komachi-dori street food

By now we'd been walking for hours in the heat and were getting hungry. We were in the mood for street food, so we made our way to Komachi-dori, Kamakura's long, lively shopping street. We spent a few hours wandering along it, taking in all the sights and smells, and sharing whatever caught our eye in between the shops.

We stopped for a lunch break first, with a Kamakura menchi katsu, a crispy fried minced beef cutlet with a cheese filling that stretched into long strings as we ate, and some tasty meat skewers. We both loved it! Later, as we wandered down the street, we snacked on a glossy candied strawberry skewer (ichigo ame) and a strawberry daifuku (a fresh strawberry wrapped in soft pink mochi). We also shared one of the warm Kamakura waffles, filled with hojicha (roasted green tea) cream.

A candied strawberry skewer (ichigo ame) and a strawberry daifuku

Shopping

We also enjoyed browsing the many souvenir shops and boutiques scattered along Komachi-dori, selling an assortment of homewares, chopsticks and gift ideas. One shop we came across stocked whole walls of chopsticks and tiny ceramic chopstick rests in all sorts of cute shapes, from cranes and cats to iconic landmarks like Mt Fuji. This is where we bought chopsticks as gifts for John's niece and nephew, and had them personalised with their names engraved.

Walls of chopsticks and tiny chopstick rests

We also stopped in at Miffy Oyatsudo Kamakura, a Miffy sweets and gift shop (oyatsu means snacks). Its window was dressed for autumn with red maple leaves and Miffy plushies. I got myself some Miffy chopsticks and a little hand towel for my collection. I also picked up a ceramic baby spoon with one of Miffy's bear friends on it for my friend Evelyn, who was expecting a baby boy, as I thought it would make such a cute keepsake.

Miffy Oyatsudo's autumn window

Heading back

Later in the afternoon, we went in search of the famous photo spot where the little Enoden train runs past with the sea behind it. We hopped between a few stops, but never quite found it!

By the time we made it back to Kamakura Station, it was dark, our legs were tired and we'd walked over 30,000 steps. While waiting for our train, we browsed a little boutique near the station selling a variety of things, including some pre-loved designer pieces, and that's where I found a beautiful butter yellow Celine purse, which I couldn't resist bringing home with me! We also shared some warm, tasty little panda-shaped cakes (ningyo-yaki) from a nearby stall to tide us over before dinner, then caught the train back to our hotel. This was definitely one of the fullest and most memorable days of our whole trip.

My verdict

Kamakura is so much more than its famous Great Buddha. Arrive early, wear your comfiest shoes and give yourself the whole day to take it all in, from peaceful temples and gardens to tasty street food and charming little shops.
