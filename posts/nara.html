Nara: Cute Deer, Ancient Temples and a Hidden Café

One of Nara's curious deer

Nara is best known for its deer, which roam freely around the city's huge park. They're everywhere: on the footpaths, on the temple steps, resting on the grass and wandering right past the old halls as if they own the place, which in a way they do. In Nara they're seen as sacred messengers of the gods, and more than 1,000 of them live in and around the park.

A deer outside one of the long red halls

They're very cute, but they're not shy at all, and they're not always as gentle as they look, especially if you have food!

Deer etiquette: Vendors around the park sell shika senbei (deer crackers), and these are the only thing you should feed the deer. I've heard that many of the deer bow their heads when they see crackers, and that you can bow back, though I didn't see it happen myself. Keep paper, tickets and snacks tucked away, or they may try to have a nibble.

The park is also home to some of Japan's most impressive and famous temples and shrines, dating back to when Nara was Japan's first permanent capital in the 700s. It's one of the most popular day trips from Kyoto or Osaka, so it's worth arriving early. We spent the whole day there, exploring around on foot.

Getting there: From Kyoto, the JR Nara Line takes about 45 minutes to JR Nara Station, and the Kintetsu line takes about 35 to 45 minutes to Kintetsu-Nara Station, which is the closer station to Nara Park. From Osaka, both JR and Kintetsu trains run straight to Nara too.

Todai-ji and the Great Buddha

Todai-ji is Nara's most famous temple. We walked up through its grounds and gardens to the Great Buddha Hall (Daibutsuden), which is incredibly grand from the outside. It's one of the largest wooden buildings in the world, and even so, the hall you see today is only about two-thirds the size of the original. It's a very popular spot, so expect plenty of tourists and school groups. You just have to accept the crowds and take it all in.

The Great Buddha Hall at Todai-ji

Inside sits the Great Buddha, a bronze statue around 15 metres tall that was first completed in 752. Around it you'll find a golden Kannon, two huge wooden guardian kings and a giant carved head that shows just how enormous these figures really are.

The golden Kannon beside the Great Buddha

Komokuten, one of the guardian kings who protect the Buddha

A giant carved head inside the hall

Towards the back of the hall, detailed models show what Todai-ji looked like at its height, including two seven-storey pagodas that once stood on either side.

Shrine paths in the forest

Nara has so many shrines and temples that there's no way to see them all in one visit, so I'd recommend picking a few and taking your time rather than rushing between them. Beyond Todai-ji, the park stretches on into a huge network of stone paths through the forest, branching off to different temples and shrines. Once we'd left the Great Buddha Hall, we wandered further into the grounds, where the crowds thinned out and the paths became mossy stone steps. We passed under a bright red torii gate guarded by a stone lion-dog (komainu), and the path beyond it disappeared into the trees.

A red torii gate and a stone komainu

Deeper in the forest, red shrine buildings sat among tall trees, and an old gate hung with a sacred rope (shimenawa) framed a small shrine on a carpet of moss. It was so peaceful, and a lovely contrast to the grand scale of Todai-ji.

Red shrine halls among the trees

An old gate with a sacred rope, framing a shrine on a mossy path

It's the kind of place where it pays to slow down and look closely, as there are little details everywhere.

A white-shelled snail and mushrooms on a mossy tree

And of course, the deer followed us here too. Some were shy, others came right up to us, and we spotted a stag with full antlers who was surprisingly vocal, calling out across the grass. Autumn is mating season, when the stags tend to get a lot noisier.

A stag with full antlers, calling across the grass

Wandering along the stone paths, we came across a quiet garden with neatly shaped trees, mossy stones and a long walkway leading up to traditional buildings.

A quiet garden of shaped trees and mossy stones

Fresh mochi

After we'd finished exploring the shrines and temples, we headed back towards the busy shopping streets to snack on a few local treats from the souvenir shops along the way. One stop we couldn't miss was Nakatanidou, a mochi shop famous for pounding its mochi fresh the traditional way. You can watch the pounding through the shop window, but it's a very popular stop, so expect a crowd gathered outside and be patient if you want a clear view.

Of course, we couldn't leave without trying some of their freshly made mochi, dusted in kinako (roasted soybean flour). Kinako isn't really for me, but trying it fresh is all part of the Nara experience, and if you're a fan of that nutty flavour, it's well worth a try!

Trays of fresh kinako-dusted mochi

A quiet wander and a hidden coffee shop

By the afternoon, we were ready to leave the crowds behind, so we wandered into the quiet back streets and stumbled on Norr Coffee Roasters by chance. From the outside, it almost looks like a house, with just a small sign and a few stepping stones to the door.

The front of Norr Coffee Roasters

Inside, it's light and Nordic-inspired, with more seating upstairs, but the real hidden gem is downstairs: a cosy indoor-outdoor nook tucked under the building, so low you have to duck to get in. With its playful hand-drawn mural and tiny garden of rocks, ferns and young trees, it felt like a secret hideaway, and the perfect place to rest and cool down after a long, hot day.

John ducking under the low ceiling

The little gravel garden

Our iced coffees were made by the friendly owner himself, who roasts all the beans right there in the shop. His passion for coffee really shines through, and sipping them in our hidden little nook was the perfect way to slow down after a busy day of exploring.

Our iced coffees

My verdict

Nara is a must if you want to see the deer up close, and they really are the stars of the show. Add in the grand Todai-ji and the peaceful forest paths and shrines, and it makes a wonderful day trip. Arrive early, pick a few sights, take your time, and finish with a quiet wander through the back streets.

Best for: animal lovers, temples and history, a spare day from Kyoto or Osaka.

Top tip: only feed the deer proper deer crackers, and keep your snacks and tickets tucked away!
